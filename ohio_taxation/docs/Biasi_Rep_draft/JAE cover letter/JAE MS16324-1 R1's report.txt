Summary

The author replicates and extends Biasi, Lafortune, and Schoenholzer (QJE, 2025, henceforth BLS), which studies the effect of school capital investments on student outcomes and house prices using a stacked dynamic regression discontinuity (DRD) design. The replication exercise reproduces the main tables and figures of BLS using both the original Stata code and an independent R implementation, confirming the robustness of the published results. The author then extends the analysis by estimating state-specific treatment effects, finding significant heterogeneity across states: while most states exhibit positive effects of bond passage on capital expenditures, the effects on test scores and house prices vary widely, with some states showing negligible or negative effects. The analysis covers 24 of the 29 states in the BLS sample.

Assessment

1. Mischaracterization of the RD approach in BLS. The paper's main methodological critique (Section 3.3) is that BLS uses the full sample of elections rather than restricting to close elections, presenting the wide distribution of vote margins (approximately -40% to +50%) as an "important identification concern." This critique mischaracterizes the BLS research design. BLS employs a standard global polynomial RD approach, controlling for cohort-specific polynomials in the vote margin, $P^g(V_{jct-k}, \delta_k^g)$, in all specifications. This is explicit in BLS (footnote 11): "Following CFR, to improve power we retain all data in estimation, rather than focusing on observations around the cutoff." The polynomial controls absorb the smooth relationship between the running variable and outcomes, so identification comes from the discontinuity at the cutoff -- this is the standard approach in the RD literature (Lee and Lemieux, 2010; Cellini, Ferreira, and Rothstein, 2010). What the author is implicitly advocating for is a local linear RD with a bandwidth restriction, which is an alternative but not a superior approach -- the tradeoff between global polynomial RD (more power, reliance on correct polynomial specification) and local linear RD (less power, more robust to functional form) is well understood. Crucially, the author does not actually implement a bandwidth-restricted version to test whether results are sensitive to this choice, instead deferring this to "future work." For this critique to be constructive, the author should implement the local linear alternative and compare results.

2. Limited novelty of the state-level extension. The paper's main claimed contribution beyond replication is estimating state-specific treatment effects (Section 4). However, BLS already present state-level estimates in their Online Appendix (Figure A39), where they replicate previously published state-specific findings (e.g., positive effects in Ohio, small effects in California, null effects in Wisconsin, Michigan, and Texas). More importantly, BLS go substantially further in explaining the sources of heterogeneity: they decompose the variance of district-specific test-score effects using a Shapley-Owen decomposition (Online Appendix Table A5) and show that most of the variation can be explained by differences in demographic characteristics and bond spending categories. The JAE paper documents state-level heterogeneity but offers only speculative explanations (differences in "pre-existing infrastructure," "policy implementation," and "local economic conditions") without any formal analysis of the drivers. The paper also does not replicate the parts of BLS that analyze heterogeneity by student income, minority share, bond category, or baseline capital stock (Sections VII-VIII of BLS), which are precisely the sections that shed light on the mechanisms behind the state-level variation. To add value beyond BLS, the author would need to either (a) offer new explanations for the state-level heterogeneity that go beyond what BLS already show, or (b) replicate the heterogeneity analysis in BLS (by income, bond category, capital stock) and show how these interact with state-level differences.
